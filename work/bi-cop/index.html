Context

Context

An analytics function at a Fortune 500 life insurance carrier with strong individual contributors but no structured mechanism for sharing BI craft, developing reporting standards, or scaling knowledge across the team. New platforms — Power BI and Microsoft Fabric — were being adopted without a learning infrastructure to support them. Governance decisions from the platform level weren't reaching the builder level with any consistency. Good individual work was staying individual.

The Problem

The Problem

The gap was structural, not technical:

  • BI analysts and engineers were learning in isolation — no shared forum existed for standards, techniques, troubleshooting, or peer review.
  • Power BI and Microsoft Fabric were being adopted without enablement support — builders discovered capabilities (and limitations) through trial and error rather than structured guidance.
  • Platform-level governance decisions weren't reaching the builder level — the rules existed but weren't being reinforced anywhere.
  • 1:1 coaching was happening informally and inconsistently — the people who happened to ask got help; the people who didn't know what to ask didn't.
  • No Power BI or Fabric curriculum existed at the team level — onboarding was ad hoc, and skill development had no visible pathway.

The missing element wasn't technical knowledge — it was an operating rhythm that turned individual learning into shared, repeatable capability.

My Role

My Role

Co-lead of the enterprise BI Community of Practice. I co-designed and co-run the bi-weekly office hours and monthly enablement sessions, coach 7–8 BI analysts and engineers on an ongoing basis, and partnered with the internal training team on Power BI and Microsoft Fabric curriculum. The CoP is a co-led program — not a solo effort — and that co-leadership is intentional: it's part of what makes it sustainable.

Approach

Approach

Four decisions shaped the program. The principle behind all of them: tools change adoption over the long run only when there's a recurring human system behind them.

  • 01

    Design for cadence, not events.

    A one-time training session doesn't change how people build. Bi-weekly office hours create a recurring space for questions, troubleshooting, and technique; monthly enablement sessions provide structured learning at a deeper level. The cadence is what makes the program a system rather than a gesture — it's still producing value after the initial enthusiasm has passed.

  • 02

    Use the CoP as the governance feedback loop.

    Platform-level governance only works if the builders who use the platform understand it. The office hours are where governance decisions become practice — where a permission model gets explained in context, where a versioning rule gets applied to a real question, where a visualization standard gets discussed rather than mandated. The CoP is the feedback loop between the platform and the people who build on it.

  • 03

    Run coaching as a parallel track, not a replacement for it.

    Group sessions set standards; 1:1 coaching is where individuals develop. The two serve different purposes and neither substitutes for the other. Coaching 7–8 BI analysts and engineers in parallel to the group program means skill gaps that aren't suited to group format still get addressed — and individuals develop at a pace the group sessions alone can't provide.

  • 04

    Build curriculum in partnership, not in isolation.

    Partnering with the internal training team on Power BI and Fabric curriculum means the enablement is institutionalized rather than dependent on whoever is running the CoP at a given moment. Content that gets reviewed, formalized, and incorporated into learning programs outlasts the individual who created it — and that's the goal.

What I Built

What I Built

  • A bi-weekly office hours cadence: open-format, question-driven sessions covering Power BI, Tableau, Microsoft Fabric, and visualization best practices — in continuous operation since launch.
  • A monthly enablement session program: structured, topic-driven sessions covering modeling techniques, governance practices, and platform capabilities at a deeper level than office hours allow.
  • A 1:1 coaching program for 7–8 BI analysts and engineers — ongoing, covering Power BI, Fabric, and reporting craft.
  • A curriculum partnership with the internal training team: Power BI and Microsoft Fabric learning content developed collaboratively and incorporated into formal training programs.
  • A feedback loop between the platform governance layer and the builder community — governance decisions that originate at the platform level get reinforced and clarified through the CoP cadence.
Outcome

Outcome

A standing operating rhythm for BI capability development across the analytics function. Governance decisions reach the builder level through the office hours cadence. New platform capabilities are absorbed through structured monthly sessions. Individual skill development runs in parallel through 1:1 coaching. The program continues to operate and produce dashboards correctly after the initial builders have moved to other responsibilities.

Reflection

Reflection

Make session output persistent from day one.

Office hours generate useful decisions — troubleshooting resolutions, governance clarifications, agreements on visualization standards. For the first several months, those outputs lived only in the memory of people who attended. A lightweight session log or decision record shared after each session would have made the knowledge persistent and accessible to people who couldn't attend. I added this later; it should have been there from the start.

Distribute facilitation earlier.

The risk in any program co-led by specific individuals is that the program and the individuals become indistinguishable. I'd focus earlier on rotating session facilitation among the BI analysts and engineers — building their confidence to lead sessions independently, rather than positioning the co-leads as the permanent hosts. The goal is a CoP that operates even if the original co-leads move on.

Platform governance is what makes reporting trustworthy; the Community of Practice is what makes the governance stick. The two are not separable — a governance model without an operating rhythm to reinforce it is just a document.
