The Dashboard No One Could Find: Reporting Discoverability at Red Hat

Context

Context

Red Hat's North American sales and finance reporting ran on a Redshift-backed Tableau environment serving leadership across product performance, revenue trends, and expenditure. The estate had grown over time — hundreds of dashboards across departments — to the point where finding the right report required already knowing it existed. Leadership pinging analytics teams for "where is the report on X" had become routine overhead absorbed by whoever happened to know the answer that day.

The Problem

The Problem

The estate had outgrown informal discovery:

  • Hundreds of dashboards across departments — no browsable or searchable surface existed anywhere in the environment.
  • Leadership depended on Slack requests and word of mouth to locate specific reports; the analytics team absorbed that overhead continuously.
  • New stakeholders had no entry point to the reporting environment at all — they didn't know what existed, let alone where to look.
  • Native Tableau Server navigation wasn't structured for how business users think about data — it reflected how content was organized, not how people looked for it.
  • There was no way to surface which dashboards were actively used, recently updated, or most relevant to a given question.

Treating this as a support problem would have meant handling individual location requests indefinitely. The right answer was to treat it as a product problem and solve for the discovery layer once.

My Role

My Role

I designed and built the discovery product: the Redshift model drawing from Tableau Server metadata and usage logs, the metadata layer, and the Tableau landing dashboard that surfaced the estate through search, browse, and usage-based navigation. The product was productionized and transitioned before my role ended in April 2022.

Approach

Approach

Four decisions shaped the product. The core principle: use what the platform already knows about itself before building anything new.

  • 01

    Use the platform's own data as the substrate.

    Tableau Server logs its own usage: which workbooks are viewed, how often, by whom, when last updated. That data was accessible in Redshift. Using what the platform already knew about itself — rather than building a separate metadata layer or requiring manual curation from dashboard owners — was what made the project feasible within the time available.

  • 02

    Treat it as a product, not a report.

    A landing dashboard that helps users find other reports isn't a report — it's a meta-product. The design problem was a discovery problem: how do business stakeholders search when they know roughly what they want, browse when they don't, and identify trustworthy content when they're uncertain either way. That framing drove every layout and interaction decision.

  • 03

    Design for three discovery patterns, not one.

    Examining how stakeholders actually found reports revealed three distinct behaviors: keyword search (a specific term, product name, or business question), structured browse (exploring by domain or function), and usage-based navigation (trusting what others most frequently open as a starting signal). The landing dashboard addressed all three rather than optimizing for one and leaving the other two unsolved.

  • 04

    Govern the metadata before handing off.

    Metadata has edge cases: workbooks without descriptions, views with low usage counts but high business criticality, permission differences that affect what surfaces for whom. Cleaning and governing the metadata layer — not just building the model — was what made the product reliable for stakeholders who weren't already familiar with the estate.

What I Built

What I Built

  • A Redshift model pulling and structuring Tableau Server metadata: workbook names, view descriptions, usage logs, update frequency, and access patterns.
  • A metadata layer treating usage frequency, recency, and stakeholder reach as first-class dimensions — the basis for surfacing relevant content rather than just listing it.
  • A Tableau landing dashboard giving the estate a single searchable entry point: keyword search, structured browse by domain, and a most-used and recently-active surface.
  • Metadata governance — description coverage, categorization, and quality — ensuring what the product surfaced was accurate and useful rather than just technically present.
  • A productionized, transitioned product handed off before role transition in April 2022.
Outcome

Outcome

Leadership gained a single entry point across the entire Tableau Server estate. Stakeholders could search by keyword, browse by domain, or navigate to the most-used content without Slack requests or support team involvement. The product was in production and transitioned before my role ended.

  • A metadata-driven landing dashboard covering the full Tableau Server estate — hundreds of workbooks made browsable from one surface.
  • Three discovery patterns in one product: keyword search, structured browse, and most-used/recently-active navigation.
  • Dependency on support teams to locate reports substantially reduced.
  • A productionized meta-product transitioned before role transition in April 2022.

Reflection

Reflection

Govern the metadata before building the product.

I addressed metadata quality after most of the build was complete. Starting with a metadata governance pass — ensuring descriptions existed, tags were consistent, and usage patterns were meaningful — would have produced a cleaner first version and avoided rework on the model after the product was already being used. Metadata quality is a prerequisite for discoverability, not an afterthought.

Build stakeholder contribution into the product from the start.

The landing dashboard surfaced what the platform already knew about itself. The next evolution would have been letting workbook owners contribute: descriptions, audience context, and tags through a governed intake process, making the product self-improving over time. I didn't have the tenure to build it; it's what I'd scope from day one next time.

The highest-leverage BI work isn't always another dashboard — sometimes it's the product that makes the existing dashboards findable. An estate of well-built, undiscoverable reports is still effectively invisible to the people who need them.
